# Teacher Collaboration Lab — Moderation Policy

**Version:** 0.1  
**Status:** Draft  
**Applies to:** Teacher Collaboration Lab Slack community  
**Moderation model:** Balanced moderation  
**Audience:** Workspace Owner, Administrators, Moderators  
**Related document:** [Community Guidelines](COMMUNITY-GUIDELINES.md)

## 1. Purpose

This policy defines how Teacher Collaboration Lab moderators respond to violations of the Community Guidelines.

The goal of moderation is to maintain a safe, respectful, professional, and productive learning environment while using proportionate responses to problems.

Moderation should:

- protect participants;
- support professional communication;
- prevent repeated disruptive behaviour;
- protect confidential and personal information;
- apply rules consistently;
- distinguish mistakes from deliberate or repeated violations;
- use the least restrictive effective response whenever appropriate.

Moderation is not intended to punish participants for ordinary mistakes, lack of digital experience, or respectful disagreement.

## 2. Core moderation principle

Teacher Collaboration Lab uses a **balanced moderation model**.

> Start with explanation and correction where appropriate, escalate proportionately when behaviour is repeated or serious, and act immediately when participant safety, privacy, security, or community integrity is at risk.

Moderators should consider:

1. severity of the incident;
2. whether the behaviour was intentional;
3. whether it has happened before;
4. actual or potential harm;
5. whether the participant corrected the problem after being contacted;
6. privacy and safety implications.

## 3. Scope

This policy applies to activity within Teacher Collaboration Lab community spaces, including:

- public Slack channels;
- private project channels managed by the community;
- threads;
- reactions;
- shared files;
- links;
- profile information used inside the community;
- community-related direct messages when they are reported to moderators.

Private conversations are not routinely monitored.

Moderators should investigate private messages only when a participant voluntarily reports a problem or provides relevant evidence.

## 4. Moderator roles

### Workspace Owner

The Workspace Owner has final responsibility for:

- community access;
- administrator permissions;
- serious disciplinary decisions;
- permanent removal decisions;
- security incidents;
- policy approval and major policy changes.

### Administrators

Administrators may:

- review reported incidents;
- contact participants;
- issue formal warnings;
- manage problematic content where Slack permissions allow;
- temporarily restrict participation where platform controls allow;
- recommend removal of a participant;
- maintain moderation records.

### Moderators

Moderators may:

- identify or receive reports of problems;
- provide informal guidance;
- request correction of inappropriate content;
- document incidents;
- escalate serious or repeated incidents to administrators.

Moderators must not independently make irreversible membership decisions unless that authority has explicitly been assigned.

## 5. Moderator conduct

Moderators must:

- remain calm and professional;
- apply the same standards to all participants;
- separate personal disagreement from rule violations;
- avoid public arguments with participants;
- protect confidential information;
- document significant moderation actions;
- escalate cases when authority or evidence is insufficient.

Moderators should not use moderation powers to:

- silence respectful criticism;
- favour particular participants;
- settle personal disagreements;
- enforce rules that have not been communicated to participants.

When a moderator is personally involved in a conflict, another moderator or administrator should review the case where possible.

## 6. Levels of violations

### Level 1 — Minor issue

Examples:

- posting in the wrong channel;
- accidental excessive tagging;
- off-topic discussion;
- unclear or inappropriate formatting;
- first-time minor breach of communication norms;
- accidental sharing of content that can be corrected without significant harm.

Typical response:

- friendly clarification;
- guidance;
- request to edit, move, or remove content.

Normally no formal warning is required.

### Level 2 — Moderate violation

Examples:

- repeated disregard of channel rules;
- repeated spam-like behaviour;
- disrespectful communication;
- repeated inappropriate tagging;
- unwanted commercial promotion;
- sharing copyrighted or restricted material after prior guidance;
- ignoring a previous moderator request.

Typical response:

- direct moderator message;
- clear explanation of the violated rule;
- request for corrective action;
- formal warning if appropriate;
- incident documentation.

### Level 3 — Serious violation

Examples:

- harassment;
- targeted insults;
- discriminatory conduct;
- deliberate disruption;
- intentional disclosure of another person's private information;
- repeated violations after formal warnings;
- sharing confidential school information;
- impersonation;
- deliberately misleading participants in a way that may cause harm.

Typical response:

- immediate intervention;
- removal or restriction of problematic content where possible;
- temporary restriction if available;
- administrator review;
- formal warning or suspension;
- possible removal from the community.

### Level 4 — Critical incident

Examples:

- credible threats;
- severe harassment;
- disclosure of credentials or sensitive personal information;
- malicious files or links;
- account compromise;
- deliberate attempts to damage the workspace;
- serious safety or security threats;
- illegal activity directly affecting community members or systems.

Typical response:

- immediate protective action;
- preservation of necessary evidence;
- escalation to Workspace Owner or administrator;
- account restriction or removal where justified;
- credential or access review where required;
- platform reporting where appropriate.

A critical incident does not require previous warnings before action is taken.

## 7. Standard moderation workflow

### Step 1 — Identify the issue

Determine:

- what happened;
- where it happened;
- which Community Guideline may apply;
- whether immediate action is necessary.

### Step 2 — Preserve minimal evidence

Record only what is necessary to understand the incident.

This may include:

- date and time;
- Slack channel;
- message link;
- short factual description;
- moderation action taken.

Avoid unnecessary copies of personal or sensitive information.

### Step 3 — Assess severity

Classify the incident as:

- Level 1 — Minor;
- Level 2 — Moderate;
- Level 3 — Serious;
- Level 4 — Critical.

When uncertain, moderators should escalate rather than overreact.

### Step 4 — Contact the participant

Where appropriate, explain:

- what the problem is;
- which rule applies;
- what corrective action is required;
- what may happen if the behaviour continues.

The purpose is correction, not confrontation.

### Step 5 — Allow correction

For minor and moderate incidents, participants should normally have an opportunity to:

- edit or remove content;
- clarify intent;
- stop the problematic behaviour;
- acknowledge the guidance.

### Step 6 — Escalate when necessary

Escalation is appropriate when:

- the violation is serious;
- behaviour continues after guidance;
- a participant refuses reasonable corrective action;
- privacy or security is involved;
- the moderator lacks authority;
- facts are disputed;
- permanent removal may be required.

### Step 7 — Document the outcome

For significant incidents, record:

- issue;
- severity;
- action taken;
- whether the participant responded;
- final status;
- any required follow-up.

## 8. Moderation actions

Moderators and administrators should use the least restrictive effective action.

Possible actions include:

1. informal guidance;
2. request to edit or remove content;
3. private moderator message;
4. formal warning;
5. temporary participation restriction where available;
6. removal from a channel where appropriate;
7. temporary suspension where technically and administratively possible;
8. removal from the workspace.

Not every incident must pass through every stage.

Serious incidents may justify immediate escalation.

## 9. Formal warnings

A formal warning should clearly state:

- the behaviour that caused concern;
- the relevant community rule;
- the expected correction;
- what may happen if the behaviour continues.

Warnings should be factual and concise.

Warnings should normally be delivered privately unless a public clarification is necessary to restore order or correct misinformation affecting the wider community.

## 10. Repeated violations

Repeated violations should be assessed based on pattern, not only individual incidents.

Escalation may be appropriate when:

- the same behaviour continues after guidance;
- several different rules are repeatedly ignored;
- the participant attempts to bypass moderator instructions;
- previous corrective actions have not been effective.

Moderators should not treat unrelated minor mistakes as deliberate misconduct without evidence.

## 11. Immediate action

Prior warning is not required when immediate action is needed to protect:

- participants;
- personal information;
- credentials;
- community systems;
- confidential records;
- workspace security.

Examples include:

- credential exposure;
- malicious links;
- threats;
- serious harassment;
- intentional publication of sensitive data.

The moderator should take the minimum necessary protective action and then document and escalate the incident.

## 12. Privacy and confidentiality

Moderation records are internal administrative information.

They must not be published in:

- public Slack channels;
- public GitHub repositories;
- public Google Drive folders;
- training materials.

Access should be limited to people who need the information for moderation or administration.

Moderation records should contain the minimum personal information necessary.

Participant applications, disciplinary records, private messages, and confidential school information must remain separate from public educational resources.

## 13. Direct messages

Moderators do not routinely inspect or supervise private messages.

A private-message incident may be reviewed when:

- a participant reports it;
- the participant provides relevant evidence;
- the behaviour affects community safety or participation.

Moderators should avoid requesting unrelated private conversations.

## 14. Evidence handling

Moderators should collect only evidence necessary for resolving the incident.

Prefer:

- Slack message links;
- short factual notes;
- timestamps;
- relevant screenshots only when required.

Avoid creating unnecessary duplicate copies of private information.

Screenshots containing personal information must be treated as confidential administrative records.

## 15. Conflict of interest

A moderator should not be the sole decision-maker when:

- they are personally involved in the dispute;
- they have a significant conflict with the participant;
- impartiality could reasonably be questioned.

The case should be transferred to another moderator, administrator, or Workspace Owner.

## 16. Appeals and review

A participant may ask for a moderation decision to be reviewed.

A review should be handled by someone other than the original decision-maker where practical.

The review should consider:

- the Community Guidelines;
- available evidence;
- severity;
- previous incidents;
- proportionality of the response.

The purpose of review is to check fairness and consistency, not automatically reverse the original decision.

## 17. Removal from the community

Permanent removal should normally be considered when:

- serious violations are repeated;
- previous interventions have failed;
- the participant deliberately harms the community;
- participant safety is threatened;
- security or confidentiality is deliberately compromised;
- continued membership presents an unacceptable risk.

Permanent removal should normally be approved by the Workspace Owner or an explicitly authorized administrator.

The reason for removal should be documented internally.

## 18. Public communication about incidents

Moderators should avoid publicly discussing individual disciplinary cases.

If clarification is needed for the community, communicate only what participants need to know.

For example:

- remind members of the relevant rule;
- clarify expected behaviour;
- explain a general moderation principle.

Do not publicly disclose:

- private messages;
- disciplinary history;
- personal data;
- confidential evidence.

## 19. Moderation records

Teacher Collaboration Lab should maintain a restricted moderation log.

Recommended fields:

| Field | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Incident ID | Internal identifier |
| Date | Date of incident |
| Channel | Relevant Slack channel |
| Category | Type of issue |
| Severity | Level 1–4 |
| Summary | Short factual description |
| Action | Moderation action taken |
| Moderator | Person handling the case |
| Status | Open / Resolved / Escalated |
| Follow-up | Any required next step |

The moderation log must not be stored in a public repository.

## 20. Moderation during the pilot

During the initial 10–20 participant pilot:

- moderation should prioritize guidance and learning;
- minor digital communication mistakes should generally be treated as learning opportunities;
- recurring problems should be documented;
- unclear rules should be identified for revision;
- participant feedback should be collected.

The pilot should also be used to test whether the moderation process is practical for administrators.

## 21. Policy review

This policy should be reviewed:

- after the first pilot cohort;
- after a serious moderation incident;
- when Community Guidelines change;
- when Slack administrative capabilities materially change;
- before substantial community expansion.

Changes should be versioned and documented.

## 22. Decision principle

When moderators are unsure what action to take, use the following sequence:

**Protect → Clarify → Correct → Document → Escalate**

The response should be proportionate to the actual risk and behaviour.

## 23. Completion criteria

The moderation system is considered operational only when:

- Community Guidelines are approved;
- this Moderation Policy is approved;
- moderator roles are assigned;
- an internal moderation log exists;
- access to the moderation log is restricted;
- administrators know the escalation path;
- the process has been tested with at least one sample scenario.

Until these conditions are met, the moderation workflow should be considered **pending implementation**.
